Foundation

What is Analogy?

Greekἀνάλογος — analogos
Derivedἀναλογία — analogia

Analogy is a method of discovery and creativity. It focuses on how two pre-existing ideas can be brought into correspondence through a change or transformation — revealing a shared relational structure that points toward a solution.

In Greek mathematics, analogy expressed the equality of ratios: a:b = c:d. In constructive geometry, it means using a known construction to approach an unknown problem — carrying the logic of the understood into what is not yet solved.

Analogical reasoning drives creative transformation of existing ideas, leading to new procedures, new constructions, and new understanding. As Pólya observed, it is one of the fundamental engines of mathematical discovery.

Key Concepts

Three Levels of Analysis

Analogy as Method

A precise transfer of relational structure from a known construction to a new problem. Through this transfer, existing mathematical knowledge becomes a generative resource — producing new procedures where direct methods have failed.

Meta-Analogy

The comparison of analogical procedures themselves. When two scholars in different traditions generate new constructions through the same kind of transformation — independently, under the same constraint — this is meta-analogy: the recurring architecture of mathematical reasoning.

Hofstadter (1995): Meta-analogies are analogies between analogies.    Burns (1996): Meta-analogies are a form of learning from analogies.

Discovery Without Direct Transmission

Many correspondences studied here do not require historical contact between traditions. Convergence arises from shared tools, foundational knowledge, and shared constraints. This is what makes the analogy structurally significant — and what makes NuminaArs genuinely innovative.

The Project

What is NuminaArs?

NuminaArs is a research and innovation project in the history, philosophy, and education of mathematics. It is a cutting-edge investigation of analogy as a method of discovery and problem-solving — studying how practitioners across history have found solutions to problems that could not be solved directly, by transforming and extending what they already knew.

Mathematics is too often presented as a sequence of fixed results. NuminaArs shifts attention to the process of formation: how new constructions emerge, how methods are adapted under constraint, and how different traditions develop comparable strategies independently.

The project draws no boundaries of time, geography, or discipline. It moves between geometry, algebra, computation, and art — allowing connections to emerge wherever genuine analogical reasoning appears.

The aim of NuminaArs is to build an innovative, applicable method for understanding mathematical discovery — making the deep logic of problem-solving visible, teachable, and useful for learners, researchers, and innovators today.

The Challenge

Five Interconnected Challenges

NuminaArs confronts several interconnected challenges — in scholarship, methodology, technology, and education. Each is real, and each shapes the direction of the project.

I

The Missing Dimension in Mathematical History

The history of mathematics has documented what was discovered, and by whom. What it has rarely asked is how — through what process of reasoning did a discovery become possible. The procedural and analogical dimension of mathematical thinking is almost entirely absent from the literature. NuminaArs exists to fill that gap.

II

What AI Cannot Do

Artificial intelligence can retrieve, recombine, and pattern-match mathematical knowledge at remarkable speed. Yet it cannot replicate what Archimedes did, what Abu al-Wafa' did, what Dürer did — reach across from the known into the unknown through a moment of genuine analogical insight. In the history and philosophy of mathematics, AI-generated responses are often plausible but unreliable. They lack the depth of reasoning, the sensitivity to historical context, and the creative human capacity for analogical discovery that serious investigation requires.

III

AI as Critical Partner — A New Methodological Challenge

NuminaArs does not reject AI — it uses it rigorously. After each stage of human investigation, AI is invited as a critical interlocutor. It may suggest connections the human researcher has not seen, propose extensions to existing discoveries, or identify weaknesses and potential errors in the analysis. But its contributions are never accepted uncritically. AI can be wrong. It can introduce errors, reinforce false patterns, or miss the historical nuance that the human scholarship has established. The challenge is not whether to use AI, but how — with discipline, scepticism, and full awareness of its limitations.

IV

Transmission Across Cultures and Time

Mathematical knowledge does not travel unchanged. As ideas move from Greek to Arabic to Latin traditions — through translation, commentary, and adaptation — they transform. Tracking analogical procedures across these traditions requires both historical and linguistic sensitivity. The challenge is to identify genuine structural correspondence without projecting false continuity.

V

Making Discovery Teachable

The deepest challenge is also the most practical: how to convert rigorous historical and analogical research into a framework that can be taught, applied, and extended. Bridging the depth of scholarship with the accessibility required for education and Ed-Tech is the ongoing work of NuminaArs.

Our Foundation

Built Alongside the MacTutor Archive

The MacTutor History of Mathematics Archive is one of the world's leading resources for the history of mathematics — biographical, encyclopaedic, and authoritative. NuminaArs takes MacTutor as a primary reference and extends in a different direction: not biographical but relational and procedural. Where MacTutor asks who discovered something and when, NuminaArs asks how — through what process of analogical reasoning did the discovery become possible. NuminaArs is conceived as a parallel and complementary companion to MacTutor, extending its reach into the procedural, creative, and educational dimensions of mathematical discovery.

Featured Case Study — Geometry

Heptagon Construction as Meta-Analogy

Abu al-Wafa' Buzjani (Islamic Golden Age, c. 990) & Albrecht Dürer (Nuremberg, c. 1525)

The regular heptagon cannot be constructed exactly using compass and straightedge — a fundamental constraint that forced practitioners across every tradition to find approximate methods. This case study examines how Abu al-Wafa' Buzjani and Albrecht Dürer, separated by five centuries and two distinct cultural traditions, each arrived at approximate constructions through the same analogical transformation of pentagon methods.

Neither construction was derived from first principles. Each was generated by carrying the logic of a known, simpler construction — the pentagon — across into the more demanding problem of the heptagon. The known method provides the framework; the new constraint transforms it. This is analogy as method: a precise transfer of relational structure from the solved to the unsolved.

The two constructions follow the same generative architecture — initial configuration, derivation of a new measure, its reuse as a generative element, and iterative completion around the circle — without any historical contact between the two practitioners.

Meta-Analogy

The correspondence is not between the constructions themselves but between the analogical procedures that generated them. Two scholars, working independently under the same constraint, reached for the same kind of transformation. This recurring architecture of mathematical reasoning is what NuminaArs calls meta-analogy.

About

The Project & Its Status

NuminaArs is an independent research and innovation project in the history, philosophy, and education of mathematics. It investigates analogy as a method of discovery and problem-solving — examining how known procedures are transformed to meet new constraints, generating new mathematical knowledge.

The project is conceived as a parallel and complementary companion to the MacTutor History of Mathematics Archive — extending MacTutor's encyclopaedic reach into the procedural, creative, and educational dimensions of mathematical discovery.

NuminaArs takes its name from the Latin numen (the animating principle, the act of willing) and ars (art, method, craft) — the idea of a skilled, intentional act of mathematical reasoning.

The project bridges academia and research communities, promoting an ESTEAM approach — Entrepreneurship, Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts, Mathematics — to foster creativity, innovation, and social impact through mathematics.

This project is conceived and directed by Parisa Kharazmi, who applies multidisciplinary expertise spanning numerical analysis, mathematical analogies, geometric patterns, scientific epistemology, history of mathematics, and digital innovation to address complex challenges.

By combining analytical and creative thinking, the project explores the intersection of mathematics with cultural, historical, and educational contexts — recognising that diverse perspectives drive mathematical understanding and innovation.

An active interest in ethnomathematics, AI philosophy, and mathematical structures in indigenous knowledge underpins a commitment to making mathematics accessible, creative, and relevant for all learners. By bridging academia and research communities, NuminaArs promotes ESTEAM to foster technology, creativity, and social impact through a multidisciplinary approach.
